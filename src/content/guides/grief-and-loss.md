Grief is the natural response to losing someone or something that matters to you. There is no right way to grieve, and no set timeline. If you are grieving, you are not doing it wrong.

## Grief after a death

When someone you love dies, grief can show up in many ways: sadness, numbness, anger, guilt, relief, or all of these in a single day. It can affect your sleep, your appetite, your focus and your energy. The National Institute on Aging describes many of these common reactions, and ways people cope, in its guide to [coping with grief and loss](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/grief-and-mourning/coping-grief-and-loss).

## Losses that don’t involve a death

Some of the hardest losses aren’t deaths at all. You might be grieving:

- a divorce or the end of a relationship
- a change in your health, or a loved one’s health
- a job, a home or a community
- a pregnancy, or a future you had hoped for
- a person who is still here but changed, for example by dementia

These losses are real, even when other people don’t recognize them. Naming them can be an important first step.

## Small things that can help

- **Go gently.** Grief takes energy. Lower your expectations of yourself where you can.
- **Let people help.** Accept a meal, a ride or a phone call, even if it feels awkward.
- **Keep a few routines.** Simple anchors, like a morning walk, can bring some steadiness.
- **Make space to remember.** Telling stories, looking at photos or marking special dates can keep a connection alive.
- **Be patient with hard days.** Holidays, anniversaries and ordinary moments can bring grief back. That is normal.

## When to reach out for support

It may help to talk with a counselor if your grief feels too heavy to carry alone, if it is getting in the way of daily life for a long time, or if you simply want a place to talk openly. You don’t need to wait until things feel unbearable.

If you are having thoughts of ending your life, please call or text **988** right away, or call **911**.
