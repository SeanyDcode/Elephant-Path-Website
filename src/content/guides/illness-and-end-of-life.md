A serious diagnosis changes things, for the person who is ill and for everyone who loves them. Whether you are living with an ongoing condition, a life-limiting disease or a terminal illness, you deserve support for the emotional side of illness, not only the medical side.

## Living with a serious illness

It is common to feel shock, fear, anger, sadness or grief for the life you had before, sometimes all at once. Many people also find unexpected moments of meaning and connection. Some things that can help:

- **Ask questions.** Understanding your illness and your options can make things feel less uncertain. It is OK to ask your care team to explain things again.
- **Share the load.** Let trusted people know what you need, whether that’s a ride, a meal or someone to listen.
- **Pace yourself.** Energy can be limited. Save it for what matters most to you.
- **Make room for your feelings.** Talking with a counselor can give you a place to say the things that are hard to say to family.

## Palliative care and hospice care

These two kinds of care are often confused:

- **Palliative care** focuses on comfort and quality of life for people with a serious illness. It can start at any stage of an illness, and you can receive it alongside treatment aimed at a cure.
- **Hospice care** focuses on comfort and support when treatment to cure or control the illness has stopped, usually near the end of life.

You can read more in [What are palliative care and hospice care?](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/hospice-and-palliative-care/what-are-palliative-care-and-hospice-care) from the National Institute on Aging, and [Palliative care in cancer](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/advanced-cancer/care-choices/palliative-care-fact-sheet) from the National Cancer Institute.

## Facing the end of life

Nearing the end of life can bring fear and questions, and also reflection, love and important conversations. People often find it helpful to:

- talk about their wishes for care with loved ones and their care team
- say the things they want to say, such as thank you, I love you or I’m sorry
- make space for what gives them comfort, whether that’s faith, music, nature or quiet company

Families and caregivers need support too, both before and after a death.
